A [glow](./shadows-and-glows.mdx) can't go past white in SDR, but an HDR
display can show brighter than white. Set `glowTuning.headroom`, and a glow
whose step would pass white also resolves an HDR value: an opaque exposure of
what SDR already paints there. The SDR value doesn't change, and exporters add
the HDR one only when you [opt in](#output-is-opt-in). Everything outside this
page is SDR; skip it unless you target HDR displays.

## When a glow gets an HDR value

[Tone runs on past 100](./okhst.mdx#tone-above-100) on the same curve: one
stop above SDR white (`Y` 2) is tone 121.98, two stops (`Y` 4) tone 144.34,
and 12 tones past white is the same `Y + 0.05` ratio as a 12-tone step
anywhere. WCAG and APCA end at white, so Glaze makes no contrast claim above
100. A glow can't be a `bg`, `fg`, `base`, or mix input, so an HDR value never
reaches the contrast solver.

A glow gets an HDR value when three things hold: its `headroom` is above 0,
its target tone (the surface's luminance tone plus `intensity × k`) passes
100, and SDR is pinned at its brightest. That means the pigment at full
opacity, or a white surface (tone 100) at opacity 0, since no pigment can lift
it. Below white nothing changes, dark-scheme glows included. A pigment that
can't lift a non-white surface at all (`reach: 0` on a gray surface, say)
paints nothing in SDR, gets no HDR value, and keeps the SDR warning. On a
chromatic surface a `reach: 0` pigment, a gray at the surface's tone, can be
slightly brighter than the surface: it then pins at full opacity and, past
white, gets an HDR value like any pinned pigment.

The resolved variant gains `hdr: { h, s, t, exposure }`. The base (`h`, `s`,
`t`) is the opaque color SDR paints, with a standard (never pastel) OKHSL
saturation. The HDR value is that base's linear light times `2^exposure`, the
gain that lands the target tone exactly; `variant.t` itself stays at most 1. The value is
opaque on purpose: it stays exact whichever way it is composited, and it maps
one to one onto native exposure APIs.

## Headroom

`headroom` counts stops above SDR white, the doublings of its luminance. It
defaults to 0 (no HDR anywhere) and must be a finite number ≥ 0, or Glaze
throws. Set it globally (`glaze.configure({ glowTuning: { headroom: 1 } })`),
in a theme's config override, on one glow's `tuning`, or on
`glaze.glow()`. A glow's `tuning` merges over `glowTuning` field by field. A
theme's own `glowTuning` replaces the global one whole, and so does a later
`glaze.configure({ glowTuning })`, so pass every field you want kept:
`glaze.configure({ glowTuning: { objectInfluence: 0.6, headroom: 1 } })`. A
`headroom` on a shadow throws: nothing goes below black.

Values above 2 clamp to 2. The warning fires once per field and value, and
only after the whole input has validated:

```text
glaze: glowTuning.headroom 4 is above the 2-stop maximum; using 2.
Headroom counts stops (doublings of SDR white's luminance), not a
  ratio: 1 stop is 2× white, 2 stops 4×.
```

The gain is capped per channel, so no linear channel of the HDR value passes
`2^headroom`. On a neutral glow that caps luminance: a 30-tone glow on white
with 1 stop of headroom ends at `color(srgb 1.3533 1.3533 1.3533)` (linear 2
per channel), tone 121.98, and warns (below). A saturated base hits the cap on
its brightest channel first, so its HDR tone can stay below 100.

How much of that a display shows depends on its headroom at the moment, which
falls as the user turns brightness up. Past it, most platforms clip the
color, which can move a tinted glow toward white, while Apple's platforms
tone-map it. A web page can't read the number. Android suggests about 1.5–2×
white for interfaces that are mostly SDR, so 1 stop is a sensible ceiling for
UI.

With headroom, a glow whose target passes white no longer logs the
[unreachable-step warning](./shadows-and-glows.mdx#unreachable-steps): its HDR
value lands the step. Only the headroom cap can leave such a step short, and
that logs its own warning. A 30-tone glow on white with 1 stop of headroom:

```text
glaze: glow "surface-flare" cannot reach a 30.0-tone step in light
  scheme even with 1 stop of headroom (reached 22.0).
Lower the intensity or raise `headroom`.
```

At the 2-stop maximum the second sentence reads "Lower the intensity;
`headroom` is already at its 2-stop maximum." A glow without an HDR value
keeps the SDR warning.

## Crossing white

A full-reach glow meets SDR at the boundary. On a gray tone-95 surface (like
`near`) a 4.9-tone glow is white at α 0.9794 and a 5-tone glow is opaque
white, both without an HDR value. At 5.1 tones it adds
`color(srgb 1.0014 1.0014 1.0014)`, 0.0046 stops.

A glow with `reach` below 1 doesn't meet it. Its pigment sits below white, so
it stays short and warns until its target passes 100, then its HDR value lands
the whole step at once. A `reach: 0.5` glow on a gray tone-90 surface
paints tone 95 for any step from 5 to 10. Past 10, its HDR value jumps straight to
tone 100 (0.2315 stops over the pigment). Keep `reach` at 1 on a glow that
may cross white.

## Extended sRGB

Glaze writes an HDR value as `color(srgb R G B)` with channels past 1: the
sRGB curve continued past SDR white, so `1` is white and one stop above it is
`1.3533`. `glaze.formatHdr(variant, 'srgb-linear')` writes the same color in
linear light (`color(srgb-linear 1.4631 1.4631 1.4631)` for `surface-glow`),
and a browser converts either one into the other exactly. The format doesn't
follow `format`: CSS clamps `oklch()` lightness at 1, and `rgb()` / `hsl()`
can't go past white.

That is all an HDR glow needs. Its value is its SDR base made brighter, so it
stays inside the sRGB gamut and is measured against SDR white, the way
displays and native APIs measure headroom. The `rec2100-pq` and
`rec2100-hlg` spaces from the CSS Color HDR draft would add a conversion, to
wider primaries and to an HDR transfer curve pinned to an assumed SDR white,
with nothing to gain for a color like this, and browsers don't support them
yet.

The same numbers are what most HDR APIs take as they are: a WebGPU `'srgb'`
canvas, Apple's extended sRGB color space, and Android's `EXTENDED_SRGB`. For
numbers instead of a string, `hdrToSrgb(variant.hdr)` returns these channels,
and `hdrToLinearSrgb(variant.hdr)` the linear ones, for APIs that take linear
light.

## Output is opt-in

Resolving fills in `variant.hdr` whenever headroom allows. Exporters leave it
out until you turn on `modes.hdr`, so every export is byte-identical until
then, headroom or not. Turn it on for the export that needs it:

```ts
const theme = glaze(280, 80, { glowTuning: { headroom: 1 } });

theme.colors({
  surface: { tone: 100 },
  'surface-glow': { type: 'glow', bg: 'surface', intensity: 12 },
  near: { tone: 95, saturation: 0, mode: 'static' },
  'near-glow': { type: 'glow', bg: 'near', intensity: 12 },
});

theme.dtcg({ modes: { hdr: true } });
theme.css({ hdr: true }); // css() has no `modes`
// Or for every export:
glaze.configure({ modes: { hdr: true } });
```

Each export then adds the HDR value next to the SDR value it already emits,
for the schemes the other `modes` include. `surface-glow` has one in the light
schemes only; on the dark surface its step stays below white. `near-glow` has
one in all four. HDR values stay literal under `splitHue`, palettes treat them
like any other entry (prefixes, the `primary` alias, collisions), and
`glaze.color()` tokens are never glows, so the HDR options don't change their
output.

The data exports (DTCG, `json()`, `tokens()`) are what native apps and
WebGPU code read. The CSS exports (`css()`, `tasty()`, Tailwind) are
experimental: no browser paints them past white yet
([Browsers](#browsers)), and their shape may change once one does. Prefer
turning HDR on per call, so a setup that only needs DTCG doesn't add the CSS
tier to every stylesheet.

### CSS blocks

`css()` takes `hdr` (default: the global `modes.hdr`) and returns four more
blocks in `hdr`. Wire each one after the SDR blocks, under the same selector
plus `(dynamic-range: high)`:

```ts
const css = theme.css({ hdr: true });
const hdr = css.hdr!;
const dark = '(prefers-color-scheme: dark)';
const more = '(prefers-contrast: more)';
const high = '(dynamic-range: high)';

const stylesheet = `
:root { ${css.light} }
@media ${dark} { :root { ${css.dark} } }
@media ${more} { :root { ${css.lightContrast} } }
@media ${dark} and ${more} { :root { ${css.darkContrast} } }

@media ${high} { :root { ${hdr.light} } }
@media ${dark} and ${high} { :root { ${hdr.dark} } }
@media ${more} and ${high} { :root { ${hdr.lightContrast} } }
@media ${dark} and ${more} and ${high} {
  :root { ${hdr.darkContrast} }
}
`;
```

The blocks are dense. Each lists every color that has an HDR value in any
scheme, and repeats a color's SDR declaration where its own scheme has none:

```css
/* hdr.light */
--surface-glow-color: color(srgb 1.1813 1.1813 1.1813);
--near-glow-color: color(srgb 1.1026 1.1026 1.1026);

/* hdr.dark */
--surface-glow-color: oklch(1 0 0 / 0.1237);
--near-glow-color: color(srgb 1.1026 1.1026 1.1026);
```

On a dark HDR display, both the light and the dark HDR blocks match. The dark
one comes later, so its `surface-glow` line puts the dark SDR value back. A
block that listed only HDR values would let the light one leak into dark.
When no glow passes white, all four blocks are empty strings.

### Tasty keys

`tasty()` adds a key under the `states.hdr` alias (default
`'@media(dynamic-range: high)'`) right after each SDR key that has an HDR
value:

```ts
theme.tasty({ modes: { hdr: true } })['#surface-glow'];
// → {
//   '': 'oklch(1 0 0 / 0)',
//   '@media(dynamic-range: high)': 'color(srgb 1.1813 1.1813 1.1813)',
//   '@media(prefers-color-scheme: dark)': 'oklch(1 0 0 / 0.1237)',
// }
```

Tasty gives later keys priority, so the order is what keeps the light HDR
value out of dark: `''`, hdr, dark, dark & hdr, hc, hc & hdr, dark & hc,
dark & hc & hdr, with only the keys that exist. Don't sort the keys if you
post-process the map. With your own aliases, set `states.hdr` next to `dark`
and `highContrast` (for example `'@hdr'`, which emits keys such as
`'@dark & @hdr'`) and register it in Tasty like the others.

### Tailwind blocks

`tailwind()` appends the same dense blocks after all its SDR blocks, under
`hdrSelector` (default `'@media (dynamic-range: high)'`), around
`darkSelector` and `highContrastSelector`:

```css
/* ...the @theme and .dark blocks, unchanged... */

@media (dynamic-range: high) {
  :root {
    --color-surface-glow: color(srgb 1.1813 1.1813 1.1813);
    --color-near-glow: color(srgb 1.1026 1.1026 1.1026);
  }
}

@media (dynamic-range: high) {
  .dark {
    --color-surface-glow: oklch(1 0 0 / 0.1237);
    --color-near-glow: color(srgb 1.1026 1.1026 1.1026);
  }
}
```

### JSON and tokens

`json()` adds a sparse `<scheme>Hdr` key after each scheme that has an HDR
value. `tokens()` adds a dense `<scheme>Hdr` map after each scheme it emits,
always present and possibly empty:

```ts
theme.json({ modes: { hdr: true } })['surface-glow'];
// → {
//   light: 'oklch(1 0 0 / 0)',
//   lightHdr: 'color(srgb 1.1813 1.1813 1.1813)',
//   dark: 'oklch(1 0 0 / 0.1237)',
// }

theme.tokens({ modes: { hdr: true } }).darkHdr;
// → {
//   'surface-glow': 'oklch(1 0 0 / 0.1237)',
//   'near-glow': 'color(srgb 1.1026 1.1026 1.1026)',
// }
```

A dense map drops into a cascade the way the CSS blocks do; the sparse JSON
keys suit code that reads one color at a time. Both carry only the string,
whose numbers are the [extended sRGB](#extended-srgb) channels. Apple's
exposure initializers take a base and an exposure, which only DTCG carries.

### DTCG

`dtcg()` and `dtcgResolver()` keep `$value` SDR: DTCG color components run
from 0 to 1. The HDR value goes in `$extensions`, only in the scheme
documents that have one. This is `light['surface-glow']`;
`dark['surface-glow']` has no `$extensions`:

```json
{
  "$type": "color",
  "$value": {
    "colorSpace": "srgb",
    "components": [1, 1, 1],
    "hex": "#ffffff",
    "alpha": 0
  },
  "$extensions": {
    "me.tenphi.glaze": {
      "hdr": {
        "exposure": 0.548987,
        "tone": 112,
        "base": {
          "colorSpace": "srgb",
          "components": [1, 1, 1],
          "hex": "#ffffff"
        },
        "value": {
          "colorSpace": "srgb",
          "components": [1.181265, 1.181265, 1.181265]
        }
      }
    }
  }
}
```

- `base` is the opaque SDR color the exposure applies to: always a DTCG sRGB
  color with `hex`, whatever `colorSpace` you export, and never pastel.
- `exposure` is the gain over `base` in stops.
- `value` is `base × 2^exposure` in linear light, written as extended sRGB:
  the numbers of the CSS string, to 6 decimals. Only its brightest component is guaranteed above 1.
- `tone` is the HDR value's tone: past 100 when the headroom covers the step,
  though a saturated base that the cap stops early can land below.

Style Dictionary and Terrazzo keep `$extensions`, but their built-in color
transforms expect components from 0 to 1, so read the extension with a
transform of your own. [Native apps](#native-apps) maps each field.

## Outside a theme

[`glaze.glow()`](./shadows-and-glows.mdx#outside-a-theme) takes `headroom` in
its `tuning` and returns `hdr` when its step passes white:

```ts
const flare = glaze.glow({
  bg: '#ffffff',
  intensity: 12,
  tuning: { headroom: 1 },
});

glaze.formatHdr(flare);
// → 'color(srgb 1.1813 1.1813 1.1813)'
```

## Where it shows

### Browsers

No browser paints a CSS color brighter than white yet. The CSS Color 4
spaces, `srgb` included, are SDR by design: values past 1 are valid, but they
are fit into SDR when painted. A neutral HDR value paints white, the same as
the SDR value of a glow pinned at opaque white (`near-glow`). A tinted one
paints a lighter, less saturated tint, or white. And on a white surface,
where SDR is transparent, the HDR value covers what is drawn under it
([Fills, not overlays](#fills-not-overlays)).

The planned way past white is the CSS Color HDR draft's `hdr-color()` (also
written `color-hdr()` there), which blends an SDR and an HDR color by the
display's headroom. No browser implements it and its name isn't settled.
`@media (dynamic-range: high)` reports what the device can do, not that HDR
is on: Low Power Mode and the brightness setting change that.

### WebGPU

A WebGPU canvas can paint past white. Chrome and Edge 129+ show an
`rgba16float` canvas configured with `toneMapping: { mode: 'extended' }`
brighter than white on an HDR display: on Windows, macOS, ChromeOS, and
Android, and on Linux since 144 with Intel Gen12+ GPUs only (not in Android
WebView). Safari 26 accepts the option, but whether it paints past white is
unconfirmed, so check on the device. Firefox has WebGPU without extended tone
mapping, so its canvases stay SDR.

The preview below is a card on a tone-92 page, its edges glowing with
`card-glow`, an `intensity: 20` glow from the card. In the light scheme the
glow's target is tone 113, past white, so its SDR value is opaque white and
its HDR value is `color(srgb 1.1972 1.1972 1.1972)` (0.593 stops). CSS draws
the halo as a `box-shadow` in the SDR value; its 40px blur fades it from about
58% at the card's edge. On an HDR display with HDR canvases, a WebGPU canvas
behind the card draws a matching halo in the HDR value instead, so the edge
glows brighter than the white card.

The canvas's default `colorSpace`, `'srgb'`, takes extended sRGB, so the
channels go in as Glaze writes them:

```ts
import { hdrToSrgb } from '@tenphi/glaze';

const { hdr } = theme.resolve().get('card-glow')!.light;
const [r, g, b] = hdrToSrgb(hdr!); // 1.1972 per channel
```

Check the mode after `configure()`: where a browser doesn't support HDR
canvases, `context.getConfiguration().toneMapping?.mode` isn't `'extended'`,
and Chrome 129 and 130 have no `getConfiguration()`. `'extended'` means only
that the browser supports HDR canvases, not that the screen shows them, so
the preview also checks `(dynamic-range: high)` before it replaces the CSS
halo. A page can't read the display's headroom, so keep `headroom` modest.

### Native apps

Read the [DTCG export](#dtcg), which carries every field these take.

- **Apple** (iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS, visionOS 26):
  `UIColor(red:green:blue:alpha:exposure:)`, or its `NSColor` twin, with
  `base.components` and `exposure`, reproduces Glaze's value. In SwiftUI,
  apply `.exposureAdjust(exposure)` to a `Color(.sRGB, …)` made from `base`.
  A view shows it brighter than white only once its layer opts in: set
  `layer.preferredDynamicRange` to `.automatic`, `.constrainedHigh`, or
  `.high`, since the default `.standard` tone-maps it into SDR. Where the
  system limits headroom it tone-maps the color, which isn't Glaze's SDR
  value (often translucent), so keep both tokens and switch yourself.
- **Android** (14 and later): set the window's color mode to
  `COLOR_MODE_HDR`, and on Android 15 its `desiredHdrHeadroom` to the
  brightest linear channel you draw. `Color.pack(r, g, b, 1f, space)` takes
  `value.components` as they are, with
  `ColorSpace.get(ColorSpace.Named.EXTENDED_SRGB)` as the space; in Compose,
  `Color(r, g, b, 1f, ColorSpaces.ExtendedSrgb)`. Int colors, `<color>`
  resources, and Compose text colors can't carry HDR.
- **Windows:** Direct2D, Win2D, and other FP16 scRGB surfaces take linear
  light where 1 is 80 nits, not SDR white. Multiply the linear channels
  (`hdrToLinearSrgb()`, or `value.components` decoded) by the display's SDR
  white level in nits divided by 80, at run time.
- Flutter and React Native: no HDR color path in their own drawing has been
  verified, so use the SDR value there, or draw the glow in a native view
  with the Apple or Android APIs above.

## Fills, not overlays

Where the HDR value clamps, as it does in CSS today, it paints an opaque
color: white, for a neutral glow. That matches SDR wherever SDR is already
opaque (`near-glow`). On a tone-100 surface it doesn't: SDR is transparent
there, and the clamped value covers anything drawn between the surface and the
glow. Use HDR glows for fills (a card's or button's own background), edge
highlights, and `box-shadow` colors, never for a state layer drawn over a
label.

## Power and accessibility

- HDR is an enhancement. The design must work in SDR, and it does: the SDR
  value is what every other display already shows.
- Don't animate or flash HDR values. A pulsing glow past white is a large
  luminance swing, and HDR flash thresholds now appear in the WCAG 2.3.1
  Understanding draft and ITU-R BT.1702-3.
- Brighter-than-white pixels cost power, and systems fall back to SDR to save
  it (iOS Low Power Mode does). Keep headroom low and HDR areas small.
